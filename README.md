# why-are-we-here
What skill allows us to turn a request into the result we actually want? I think the underlying skill is the ability to break things down and bring them together—in other words, the ability to analyze. Take a request to create “a generative poster that feels unsettling.” From an artistic perspective, you need to break down the style, composition, and structure of objects in reference images. You also need to examine what makes something feel unsettling. From a programming perspective, you need to analyze which prompts are effective, which model produces better results, and even how to use fewer tokens. All of this depends on the ability to break things down and bring them together.

I think this ability matters because, when you ask AI to do something, you use natural language, right? Many people talk to AI as though they were chatting with someone: “Hey, I want an image that feels unsettling.” But that request is clearly vague. If my boss gave me such a vague request without any further explanation, I would feel overwhelmed and have no idea what to do. That is why I think you need to break down and bring together your ideas when making a request to AI, so that the request is specific and clear enough. To me, that is how you get what you truly want.

What is the cost of not understanding the medium you use? I think it is less about possibility than potential. The more deeply you understand a medium, the better you understand what it can do.

Of all the fields in which AI is used, opposition seems especially strong in art—or, more broadly, in creative fields. When we were students, copying an essay always seemed harder to accept than copying answers to math or physics homework, didn’t it? I think people respond to AI in much the same way.

Returning to the main point, I want to discuss this question through the process of making art. Creating something involves discarding some things, keeping others, choosing one option from several, and repeating this thousands upon thousands of times. These decisions are what make up a work and determine whom it belongs to. In that sense, the creative process is a process of decision-making. The artistic and subjective qualities of an image or a novel often come from the thousands upon thousands of personal choices its creator made along the way.

I think AI-generated work is controversial because it reduces the number of decisions to a tiny fraction. If painting an image originally required tens of thousands of decisions, but using AI produces that image after only a few dozen, can it really reflect the artistry and subjectivity of the person using AI? This is especially relevant because AI models are often trained on hundreds of thousands of artworks, each created through tens of thousands of decisions by its artist. Those few dozen decisions do not show what the AI user wanted to express. Instead, they show an accumulation of decisions made by the artists whose works became training material.

Therefore, I believe the number of decisions a person makes in creating a work determines whether that work belongs to them. I do not think “understanding how the system works” directly changes who deserves credit. Rather, it helps people discover more places in the creative process where they can make decisions. As the number of decisions increases, the individuality of the work becomes more apparent, and disputes over authorship become less intense.
